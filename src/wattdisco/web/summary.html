    HR drift
    --%

    Heart rate drift (also called aerobic decoupling) shows how much your heart rate crept up against your power as the ride went on. The ride's seconds with both power and heart rate are cut into two halves, and each half gets its watts per beat: average power ÷ average heart rate. The drift is how much the second half's watts per beat fell below the first half's.

    Reading it: under 5 % on a long steady ride means your aerobic base held up well; 5–10 % is some fatigue; above 10 % the ride was beyond what your aerobic fitness carries for that long. Heat, little to drink, tiredness or a hard day before push it up too. A negative number means your heart rate fell against power, usually because the second half was harder or the first half held a long warm-up.

    It means most on a steady ride of an hour or more at endurance pace. On interval workouts the two halves hold different efforts, so compare it only with rides of the same workout. Needs at least 2 minutes in each half.

    AeT · α1
    --bpm

    Aerobic threshold from heart rate variability (DFA α1). When you ride easy, the time between heartbeats varies in a complex, correlated way and α1 is around 1. As effort rises the pattern turns random and α1 falls towards 0.5. Where α1 crosses 0.75 lines up with the aerobic threshold (first ventilatory threshold).

    How: the RR intervals of your main strap; beats outside 300–2000 ms or 20 % off the one before are dropped as artefacts. Every 20 s a 2-minute window (at least 100 beats, under 5 % artefacts) gets α1: the beat series is summed up, cut into boxes of 4–16 beats, a straight line is taken out of each box, and α1 is the slope of the remaining fluctuation against box size on a log–log scale. A straight line through α1 against heart rate (and power) over all windows gives the heart rate and power where it is 0.75.

    Shown only when the ride has windows on both sides of 0.75 and α1 clearly falls as heart rate rises (correlation below −0.5). A ride from easy to hard shows it best. It's an estimate, not a lab test.
